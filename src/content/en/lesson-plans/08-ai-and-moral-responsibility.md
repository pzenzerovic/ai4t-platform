## Objective

Students compare questions and answers they wrote themselves with those produced by an AI assistant, and can argue why the responsibility for what is finally submitted stays with the person who submits it.

## Preparation

- A digital classroom, one computer per pair of students, with access to Microsoft Copilot.
- The task brief from the international project: 25 questions across five subject areas.
- Paper and pens for the first round — the first set of questions is written without any tool.
- A shared document or board where the two sets of questions can be placed side by side.
- Mixed groups: the source workshop brought together two grade 8 classes and one grade 4 class.

## Lesson flow

1. **Writing the questions the standard way** — Students compose 25 questions across the five required areas using their own knowledge and the usual sources, without an AI tool.
2. **Writing the same questions with Copilot** — Students give Copilot the same brief and collect the questions it produces.
3. **Comparing the two sets** — The sets are placed side by side and compared on three criteria: the quality of the question, its factual accuracy, and whether it is ethically acceptable to ask it.
4. **Answering through independent research, then comparing with Copilot** — Students research the answers to their questions themselves, then compare their answers with the ones Copilot gives, and note where the two differ.
5. **Discussion on moral responsibility, and submission** — A closing discussion on who carries responsibility for an answer that turns out to be wrong. The answers the students arrived at through their own research are the ones submitted to the project.

## Learning outcomes

- Students can compose quiz questions in a given subject area to a set standard.
- Students can produce the same task with an AI assistant and compare the two results.
- Students can assess a question for factual accuracy and for ethical acceptability.
- Students can research an answer independently and compare it with an AI-generated answer.
- Students can argue why responsibility for a submitted answer rests with the person and not with the tool.

## Notes

- The workshop was run as part of an international project ("This is our TIME"); the annexes list a certificate of participation and 5th place worldwide.
- The mixed age range (grade 4 alongside grade 8) worked as a feature of the workshop, not a problem: younger students test whether a question is understandable, older ones test whether it is accurate.
- The source records the sequence of work but not how long each step took, so no per-step timings are given here. A teacher repeating the workshop should plan the two writing rounds as the longest blocks.
- Note that the deliberate order matters: students write without the tool first. Reversing the order removes the point of comparison.
