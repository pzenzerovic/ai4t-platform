## Objective

Students can explain that an artificial intelligence system learns from examples given to it by people, and that the quality of those examples decides whether it recognises an object correctly.

## Preparation

- Printed sets of traffic sign cards, one set per group — STOP signs together with similar signs that are not STOP (for example "No entry").
- Harder examples prepared in advance: rotated signs, partly covered signs, signs in poor visibility.
- Printed student worksheet, one per student (sections: *Observe and think*, *Training the AI*, *Can AI make mistakes?*, *Hard examples*, *Conclusion*, *Bonus*).
- Optional: computer and projector with Teachable Machine open. The source report marks this phase as optional, "if technology is available".
- Students seated in groups of 3–4.

## Lesson flow

1. **How do you recognise something you know?** · 5 min — Guided discussion: how do you know that an animal is a dog, or that a sign is a STOP sign? The teacher uses the answers to explain that AI does not think like a human but looks for patterns in data that people give it.
2. **Main activity: "You are the AI"** · 20 min — Each group gets a set of sign cards. The groups select the examples that represent a STOP sign, name the key features (shape, colour, text, context) and separate correct from incorrect examples. The teacher plays the role of the AI system and deliberately makes classification errors (confusing STOP with a similar sign). Harder examples are then introduced — rotated, partly covered, poorly visible — to show the limits of the system.
3. **AI demonstration** · 10 min *(optional, only if the equipment is available)* — The teacher demonstrates Teachable Machine: the model learns from the examples entered and then gives predictions. Students watch how the behaviour differs for clear and for ambiguous data.
4. **Reflection and discussion** · 5 min — Three questions: Can AI make mistakes? Why do mistakes happen? Should we always trust AI? Students complete the *Conclusion* section of the worksheet.

## Learning outcomes

- Students can explain that artificial intelligence learns from examples rather than thinking independently.
- Students can identify the key features needed to recognise an object.
- Students can recognise that AI systems make mistakes.
- Students can explain that the quality of input data directly affects how AI performs.
- Students demonstrate basic critical thinking about the use of AI in everyday life.

## Notes

- Observed during the pilot: the role-play element ("the teacher is the AI") was the most effective part for understanding an abstract concept; engagement was high during group work and during the simulated errors.
- Difficulties reported: the abstract concept of AI is hard at first, prior knowledge varies within the class, and there is little time for more advanced examples. Guided questioning and simplified explanations were used to address this.
- Step 3 can be dropped without breaking the lesson; the remaining three steps then take 30 minutes.
